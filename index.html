WHY THIS CAME FROM THE MEDICAL SIDE

INTELDAS™ did not begin by teaching AI to write a better demand.

It began with a medical-evidence problem.

For more than four decades, Dr. Frank Liberti's work has operated at the intersection of clinical injury evaluation, medical evidence and personal-injury litigation.

More than three decades ago, that work included serving as a medical expert in New Jersey Supreme Court litigation addressing the objective medical evidence necessary to establish qualifying injury under the state's tort threshold—the Verbal Threshold—where the injury claim required proof of significant, consequential and permanent injury.

The lesson was consequential:

The injury alone wasn't enough. The medical evidence had to demonstrate its significance and consequences.

That same problem now exists in a different technological environment.

Today's legal AI can perform extraordinary work with the information it receives.

But AI still cannot reason from consequential medical evidence that was never developed into the case.

INTELDAS grew from more than 13 years of engineering medical-legal forensic methodologies and clinical decision intelligence around that problem.

The result is not simply another demand generator.

It was built to solve the medical-evidence problem before the demand is written.

AN INVENTOR'S PERSONAL NOTE

For years, I kept asking myself a simple question: Why hadn't the legal-AI and demand-formulation companies already solved this?

I believe part of the answer is that we approached the same claim from opposite directions.

Most legal AI naturally begins with the record it receives:
RECORD
↓

↓

I came to the problem from the medical side:
INJURY
↓

↓

↓

That difference matters.

If you begin with the existing record, the natural question is:

“What can AI find, analyze and produce from this evidence?”

My question was different:

“What consequential medical evidence should exist to properly demonstrate this injury—but was never developed in the first place?”

That question ultimately led to INTELDAS™ and FEDI™.

Not simply another way to analyze the evidence.

A proprietary, patent-pending system designed to develop supportable medical-forensic intelligence that wasn't adequately present in the original record.

Perhaps that's why the missing-evidence gap was easier for me to see from the medical side.

I wasn't trying to teach AI to write a better demand.

I was trying to make sure the demand had better evidence to work with.
